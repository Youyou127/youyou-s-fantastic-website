WEEK 01WELCOME WEEK

Arriving, Listening, Exploring

My first week in Leeds was shaped less by workshops and more by lectures, welcome meetings and the experience of finding my place in a new academic environment. Coming from China to study Digital Media in the UK, even ordinary routines felt unfamiliar. I was listening, observing and trying to understand how learning and student life might work here.

Welcome Week gave me opportunities to explore beyond the classroom. I attended introductions, looked into student societies and became curious about activities such as Give It a Go—a chance to try something new without needing to commit straight away. I was still adapting, but each small encounter made the university feel a little less unknown.

There was no workshop yet, which left me wondering what it would be like to learn by making. I felt curious about the practical sessions ahead and slightly unsure of what to expect. For now, this week was about arriving, noticing and giving myself time to settle in.

WEEK 02FIRST WORKSHOP

Choosing a Platform, Learning by Making

This week we began our website workshop. Before class, we were asked to download Visual Studio Code (VS Code), which we would use to create HTML and CSS files. We were also given a Codecademy reading task, including the sections “Site Structure” and “A Closer Look at CSS”.

We explored two possible routes for building a portfolio: CampusPress, which uses a theme, and GitHub Pages, which allows more freedom through HTML and CSS. I chose GitHub Pages. The choice felt exciting, but the first practical steps were also a little daunting.

I was grateful to the tutors who moved around the room, checked in with us and answered questions. At the same time, I realised how important preparation is. Without previewing the material or having any experience with the tools, it was difficult to follow every step at the pace of the workshop.

My friend and I worked through the language barrier together—it is something we are still improving, haha—while trying to keep up with the process. In the end, we successfully created our websites. The tutor made the session lively, explained the steps in an engaging way and even gave us a round of applause when we finished. I really enjoyed the supportive atmosphere.

After class, my friend and I went to a café. We talked about possible next steps for each other’s websites and previewed the next class material. That quiet moment helped me feel settled, and I left looking forward to the next workshop.

SMALL WIN

My first website is online—and I already have ideas for what it could become.

WEEK 03WEB SCRAPING

Finding the Right Data in the Page

This week’s workshop introduced web scraping: collecting information from a web page by identifying the elements that contain it. We used the Web Scraper extension in Chrome, and I chose the BBC iPlayer website as my starting point. I tried to collect programme information, including titles and short descriptions.

My first attempt returned only a small amount of information. The tutor explained that I had selected the wrong elements on the page. I had not yet found the parts of the page that contained the information I wanted. This showed me that scraping is not simply a matter of clicking a button: I also need to understand how a website organises its content and choose the right elements carefully.

The tutor also recommended Real Python’s introduction to web scraping with Python. I tried this approach too. It was more challenging than using the browser extension, but it also seemed more precise because I could specify more carefully what I wanted to collect. I can see the value of learning it, even though I need more practice.

I left the workshop thinking about the difference between seeing information on a website and turning it into a dataset. The tools can help collect what the code makes visible, but my choices—what to select and what to leave out—also shape the result.

WEEK 04DATA, POWER & CLASSIFICATION

Turning an Interest into a Research Question

This week’s lecture and workshop asked us to think about data not as a neutral collection of facts, but as something shaped by choices: what we count, how we name it and whose experiences become visible. In our group discussion, we focused on international students and generative AI. Our question was: How does generative AI help international students overcome language barriers in their studies?

We began to map what we might want to understand: whether students use AI to translate, read, write or speak; which tools they mention; how often they use them; and whether they find them helpful. These categories gave our broad interest a possible structure, while also making us ask what each category might leave out.

Our brainstorm raised ethical questions too. We noted the importance of respecting users’ privacy and avoiding personal information, especially if using social media posts. We also recognised a gap: posts on social media may not represent the experiences of all international students. Academic research and other online sources could offer different perspectives, though each source would have its own limitations.

The exercise helped me see that a research question is only a beginning. Decisions about sources, variables and labels can influence the picture a dataset creates. Before collecting anything, we needed to be clear about what we wanted to learn and whose experiences our approach might miss.

QUESTION TO KEEP

What becomes visible when we turn people’s experiences into categories—and what disappears?

WEEK 05DATA COLLECTION & VISUALISATION

When the Search Does Not Match the Question

This week we worked towards collecting online material for our group question and considered how it could be structured for analysis and visualisation. We hoped to understand how international students use generative AI to navigate language barriers, but finding enough relevant material within a comparable timeframe proved difficult.

We turned to secondary sources, yet useful evidence was limited and its accuracy was not always clear. Online information is unstructured: the same experience can be described in different ways, while search results depend on the terms and platforms we choose. At first, we were looking too narrowly, especially by relying on Reddit.

During the workshop, I shared a reflection on this problem. I suggested refining our keywords so that searches could focus on specific kinds of content, while also widening the search across platforms and source types instead of limiting ourselves to Reddit. This did not solve every difficulty, but it helped me see search as an iterative process: test a strategy, notice what it misses and adjust it.

The experience made the limitations of online data tangible. A lack of visible posts does not mean that students do not use AI or face language barriers. It may mean that their experiences are shared elsewhere, described differently or not made public at all. Any later visualisation will need to be read alongside these limits.
